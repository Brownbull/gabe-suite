the strategist power user · "where is the edge?"

Every edge on this page is public information: printed on a token, dealt into a deck, or drawn on the board. The edge is not the number — it is being the only one at the table who computed it. Five panels below, one per edge, each wearing the icon of the sphere it exploits; the page ends on the opening algorithm, because four of the five edges are spent before the first road exists.

Edge 1 · the pip edge — the dice are not fair, they are triangular

Two dice make 36 ordered outcomes and stack them into a triangle. A 6 or an 8 arrives five times as often as a 2 — and Catan prints this on the tokens as pips, so the edge is sitting face-up on the table from setup.

Edge 2 · the corner edge — 54 intersections, and they are not equal

A settlement corner touches up to three hexes, so a corner's income is the sum of its pips. The best corner a board can offer is 6-8-5 or 6-8-9: fourteen pips, a card on 14 of 36 rolls. And every settlement placed voids its neighbouring corners by the distance rule — the good corners are a shrinking resource that only setup can buy.

Edge 3 · the harbour edge — the ratio is a property of the board

Everyone trades the bank at 4:1. A harbour is a permanent discount you buy with placement: 3:1 anywhere-generic, 2:1 in one resource. A 2:1 that matches your heaviest pips doubles the throughput of every surplus card — it turns a monoculture corner from a weakness into a mint.

Edge 4 · the robber edge — the only attack, and it costs nothing

The robber is the base game's one aimed weapon, priced at zero cards. Parked on a leader's 6 or 8 it silences 5/36 of their production for as long as it sits — aim it at the engine, never at the hand. Fourteen knights in the deck mean it never stops moving.

Edge 5 · the deck edge — a second scoreboard the table cannot read

The development deck is 25 cards with a published composition, so every draw has odds the table can know and mostly doesn't: 56% knight, 20% a hidden victory point. A player "stuck on 8 points" holding two VP cards is not stuck — the deck is where leads hide.

What this seat cannot price

The trade table. A 2:1 harbour has arithmetic; a persuasive offer does not — there is no /36 for "she always trades with whoever is losing". That edge belongs to the social sphere, resists every number on this page, and is named here rather than dressed in a fake one. For whose question this page answers — and the five other seats — see the Catan index.

1 · pip count — the board's income statement

Every token's pip count is its ways-to-roll: 6 and 8 carry five pips (and red ink), 5 and 9 four, down to one pip on 2 and 12. Reading the board as pips instead of numbers is the whole trick — a "nice looking" 11-wheat hex is two pips, a fifth of a 6-brick. The pip map is the same for everyone; the edge is that most tables never draw it.

2 · corner shortlist — income per intersection

Sum the pips of a corner's up-to-three hexes: 14 is the board's ceiling (6·8·5), 10+ is strong, 7 is average. The shortlist shrinks as others place — the distance rule voids up to three corners per settlement — so the ranking is worth having BEFORE the snake reaches you, not during your turn.

3 · the two placements — pips, then diversity, then harbours

Tiebreaks in order: total pips first; then resource spread — five resources exist and a city needs ore, so an ore-less opening is a ceiling on the whole game; then harbour reach for the 2:1 that matches your surplus. The second settlement pays its adjacent hexes immediately — the only free cards in the game, and the reason the snake's double placement matters.
